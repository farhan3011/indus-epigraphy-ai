# Ship five cheap Indus demos next

The best next steps are demos that reuse the two assets already built: the Kneser-Ney (KN) trigram scorer from Milestone 3 and the permutation harness from Milestone 4. Four of the five top items need only a day or two of CPU-only work. Each also turns an abstract result into something a viewer can see. On the Milestone 3 side, do joint multi-gap beam search with unknown-length gaps, leave-one-out saliency, and parallel-text retrieval, all in one interactive page. On the Milestone 4 side, do a hill-climb "ceiling" experiment and a Linear B positive control. The ceiling experiment tests whether a fitted key can match the claimed Sanskrit table. The positive control tests whether the harness can detect a real decipherment at all. That second question matters most. Milestone 4's null result is only informative if the harness has power, and the Old Tamil check showed 0% power. Fixing this is the highest-value work on the decipherment side. Deep-model and lost-language-system work ranks last, because corpus size (about 1,500 texts, about 5,700 tokens) caps what those methods can show. Evidence for several items is from search summaries or memory, and this is flagged below.

## Milestone 3: borrow the interface, not the architecture

Ithaca and Aeneas were trained on tens to hundreds of thousands of texts, so their architectures do not transfer to Indus. Their decoding and interface ideas do, because those run on any scorer. Ithaca returns top-20 ranked candidates and saliency maps, and raised historians' restoration accuracy from 25% to 72% ([Ithaca, Nature 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8907065)). Aeneas restores gaps of unknown length, scoring 73% top-20 for gaps up to 10 characters but 58% when length is unknown. It also retrieves textual parallels, which historians found useful starting points in 90% of cases ([Aeneas abstract](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:f4cedd8c-3ba2-40bd-afac-d7b1e31eefd1), [DeepMind blog](https://deepmind.google/blog/aeneas-transforms-how-historians-connect-the-past/)). The 73%/58% figures come from the blog and should be checked against the paper.

Three items follow directly. First, joint beam search over several masked positions: with 377 signs and a beam of 20, the KN-plus-neural mixture scores whole fills instead of independent per-gap top-k lists. Extending it to lengths 1..L with a length-normalised score and a corpus length prior (median about 5 signs) mirrors the Aeneas unknown-length setting. Evaluation is joint exact-match when masking 2-3 signs, plus the length-known versus length-unknown drop. Second, saliency: under KN, leave-one-out on context signs gives a faithful attribution. Colouring the sign strip by it is an easy way to show what drives a prediction. Third, retrieval: TF-IDF over sign n-grams, shown with site and object metadata, needs no learned embeddings and fits 1.5k texts.

A fourth, smaller item is an anomaly flag from the minimum conditional probability per text. It needs a synthetic-corruption test, such as swapping two signs, before it counts as a result. Treat its value as an epigraphic tool as speculative; it is inspired by null-model work rather than a published method.

## Milestone 3: position the numbers honestly before adding models

The 42.2% top-1 should not be set beside earlier Indus figures. Rao-group bigram restoration reports about 75%, but trained on texts with damaged signs removed, and a student positional-Markov fill-in reports about 63% ([Rao et al., arXiv](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/0901.3017), [Yadav slides](https://www.imsc.res.in/~sitabhra/meetings/bitsscripts24/Nisha_Yadav_Lecture.pdf), [Emerging Investigators](https://emerginginvestigators.org/articles/22-256)). A cheap, honest addition is to re-score the model on an EBUDS-style clean subset and state the protocol difference. The calibration and conformal sets already built then become the differentiator: none of those papers offers coverage guarantees.

For model gains, the n-gram family wins at about 7k tokens, which matches the 42% KN versus 32% neural result. Candidate add-ons are a skip-bigram and position-in-text interpolation, class-based back-off using induced positional classes, and a kNN cache over near-duplicate seals. The cache may help most, since seal repeats exist. These come from memory (Brown 1992, Kuhn and De Mori 1990 and similar), not retrieved sources. Differences under about 2 points are noise at this corpus size, so use the existing leave-one-out protocol with bootstrap CIs and expect small, possibly null, gains. Positional class induction and branching-entropy sign-group segmentation are the moderate-effort extensions with visible output (a class table, segmented seals). Site-of-origin prediction and allograph merging have no Indus precedent found and are heavily imbalanced or unvalidated, so keep them last.

## Milestone 4: establish power and the ceiling before claiming more

The harness's p of roughly 0.06 against frequency-matched keys says Yajnadevam's table is not distinguishable from random. A skeptic will ask whether the harness could ever tell. The hill-climb ceiling answers half of that. Reassign one sign's sound at a time, optimise the existing trigram-minus-unigram score from random starts, and report the claimed key's percentile within the optimised distribution plus how many signs move when starting from the claimed key. A rough unicity estimate (my own back-of-envelope: about 400 signs times log2 of about 25 consonant classes gives about 1,900 bits; at 1-2 bits of redundancy per symbol, roughly 1,000-2,000 symbols) puts the 5,700-token corpus near the point where a many-to-one key is determined. Zipfian sign frequencies make that bound optimistic, so a high fitted score is expected and proves little. This is inference, not literature. Related work shows that weak language models give wrong "best" keys: a 3-gram optimum had 38.6% error versus 4.13% with a 6-gram ([Nuhn et al., ACL 2013](https://aclanthology.org/P13-1154/); homophonic extension in [EMNLP 2014](https://mirror.aclweb.org/emnlp2014/papers/pdf/EMNLP2014184.pdf)). The animated "optimise a random key and watch it pass the claimed score" page is the most legible overfitting demo.

The positive control answers the other half. Linear B is deciphered, uses roughly 87 syllabograms, and its tablets are short administrative lists with many names, which resembles Indus. Apply true sign values, reduce to consonant skeleton, score with a Greek trigram model, and run the same shuffled-key null. The true key should beat frequency-binned shuffles. If it does, the Indus null gains weight; if not, the harness needs repair first. Pair this with voicing-neutral scoring, collapsing stops to place classes, which is the proposed fix for the Tamil 0% power. Data is bundled in the NeuroDecipher repo, but that repo shows no licence, and DAMOS and LiBER are reportedly non-commercial per a third-party README ([GitHub](https://github.com/j-luo93/NeuroDecipher), [HF commit](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Nacryos/ancient-scripts-datasets/commit/105ea5a87e3bf2bb5e19554d8ead929352240dc8)). Read the official licences first, fetch data at runtime, and keep it out of the repo.

Two quick additions fit in under a day each. A held-out split (optimise on half the inscriptions, score the other half, also split by site) tests overfitting directly: a real key should transfer, an overfit one should fall toward the null. A score-gain-per-bit measure compares claims fairly by counting key degrees of freedom. These are standard cross-validation practice rather than published diagnostics for many-to-one keys. Reporting critiques of the unicity claim are partisan web material only, and I found no peer-reviewed rebuttal, so do not cite them as established.

## Conclusion

The two milestones fit together as a pair. Milestone 3 offers a scorer that is honest about its uncertainty, and Milestone 4 offers a test that is only as strong as its demonstrated power. Combining them suggests a stronger use of the language model: score claimed decipherments with the same calibrated, leave-one-out machinery, and require any claim to beat both the fitted-key ceiling and held-out transfer. The Linear B control is the pivotal experiment: success makes the Indus null a real finding, and failure redirects effort.

Evidence limits apply. Search was shallow (about six calls on the model side, ten on the harness side), the 2026 Indus preprints were not read, and no earlier Indus run of the lost-language systems was located. Full-system decipherment tools such as NeuroDecipher need a related-language lexicon Indus lacks, so only their language-closeness idea is reusable.
