# Replicate the bigram, restrain the claims

> **Correction, 2 October 2026 (after running the code).** This report says the project's IM77 export cannot reproduce EBUDS. It can. Treating each inscribed line as a unit, dropping lines with an illegible or doubtful sign, keeping only lines that are the sole line on their side (`line == 0`), and removing duplicates gives exactly 1,548 texts, 7,000 tokens and 377 signs, and the 40 dropped signs match the published list. The filter variants tabulated below missed that combination. See `reports/milestone_2/milestone_2_report.md`. The exploratory direction figures quoted below were also superseded by that run.

Milestone 2 should be run as a like-for-like replication of Yadav et al. 2010 on an EBUDS-style subset of IM77, using a hand-written Witten-Bell bigram model, with a physical-position test of writing direction and conclusions that stop at "sequential and positional regularity". The published targets are perplexity **68.82 / 26.69 / 26.09 / 25.26 / 25.26** for n = 1..5, unigram entropy **6.68 bits**, bigram mutual information **2.24 bits**, and **82 versus 23 signs** covering 80% of text beginners versus enders, all on 1,548 texts and 7,000 tokens ([Yadav et al. 2010](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0009506)). The project's export does not reproduce that subset (its filters give 1,291 to 1,849 units, never 1,548), so these numbers are bands to land near, not values to match, and the report must say "EBUDS-style filter" and publish its own funnel. NLTK cannot be used for the model: its Witten-Bell ends in an unsmoothed unigram and returns infinite perplexity whenever a held-out sign is absent from training, which on this corpus is every fold. Forward-versus-backward bigram perplexity is not a direction test, because the two are equal by the chain rule; direction has to be tested on physical left and right ends after undoing the concordance's normalisation. The often-quoted "74% restoration accuracy" is a 90%-probability-mass hit rate, not top-1 accuracy. Fifteen years of criticism, plus two 2026 preprints, establish that none of these statistics separates language from structured non-language, so the milestone may claim structure and predictability and nothing about language or meaning.

## Yadav 2010 is the target; Rao 2009 is a cross-check with a different estimator

Every headline number in the literature comes from one small subset. Yadav et al. computed everything on EBUDS, built with SRILM using **Witten-Bell smoothing with Katz backoff**, n-gram orders 1 to 5, and start and end tokens, with the log-likelihood-ratio pair tests done in the NSP toolkit ([Yadav et al. 2010](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0009506)). Rao et al. 2009 used the same 1,548 lines and 7,000 sign occurrences but a different estimator: a first-order chain with **modified Kneser-Ney** smoothing, 417 signs plus an end-of-text token in a 418 x 418 matrix, a start prior instead of a start token, and signs fed right to left ([Rao et al. 2009, PNAS](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~rao/PNASIndus.pdf)). The Science paper defines its entropies in nats and prints no numeric Indus value, only curves ([Rao et al. 2009, Science](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~rao/ScienceIndus.pdf)). Because the milestone specifies Witten-Bell, Yadav is the replication target and Rao supplies single-cell checks on the transition matrix.

Pair strings in Yadav's tables are in reading order, so "267, 99" means 267 is read first ([Yadav et al. 2010](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0009506)). Several of these figures lock together and make better tests than any single value. The pair 267,99 occurs 168 times and the third-ranked sign occurs 211 times, so the unsmoothed P(99 | 267) is 0.80, which is the "approximately 0.8" Rao reports; that identification of Rao's glyph ranks with sign numbers is an inference from the two papers, not something either states. Cross-entropy is log2 of perplexity, so one row of Table 1 suffices. Sign 342 at 715 of 7,000 tokens is the 10% share both papers quote ([Sinha et al. 2009](https://aclanthology.org/W09-3202.pdf)). These structural checks survive modest differences in filtering; the exact totals do not.

Rao's PNAS paper adds three more checkable results. Among signs occurring at least 20 times, the ten most likely enders have end-of-text probabilities **between 0.87 and 0.39**, and the most frequent sign is followed by end-of-text with probability 0.74. One frequent sign occurs as a doubled pair in **33 of the 58 inscriptions** containing it. And the eight complete West Asian texts score a median likelihood of **6.40e-13 against 1.12e-7** for 100 held-out, length-matched Indus-region texts ([Rao et al. 2009, PNAS](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~rao/PNASIndus.pdf)). Two of those eight likelihoods are exactly zero because the texts contain signs unseen in training, so that model did not smooth at the vocabulary level; it is a published instance of the zero-probability failure discussed below, and a Witten-Bell model with a uniform base case will give small non-zero values instead.

Four published details need loose tolerances. First, **the 74% is not top-1 accuracy**: a hit is counted when the deleted sign falls "under 90% area of the cumulative probability curve", with signs dropped at random from each test fold and the procedure repeated 100 times ([Yadav et al. 2010](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0009506)). A 2026 preprint that re-implemented the protocol on a synthetic corpus of similar size got **84.88% by that criterion and 24.84% top-1** ([Raghavendra 2026, preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.02999)), so a student reporting top-1 should expect a number far below 74% and should report the mean candidate-set size beside any 90%-mass figure. Second, the train/test split behind the perplexity table is not stated; the 5-fold 80:20 protocol is described only for restoration. Third, the entropy figures differ between versions: the PLoS text gives 6.68 and 2.24 against a random baseline of 8.56, while the arXiv v1 body text says 6.3 and 2.3 and its tables give random values of 8.70 and 8.73 ([arXiv:0901.3017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0901.3017)). Fourth, the unigram entropy (6.68 bits) exceeds the held-out unigram cross-entropy (6.10 bits), which is impossible over a common event space; the likely cause is that the perplexity counts the highly predictable end-of-text token and the entropy does not. That explanation is an inference, so compute both variants. No independent re-derivation of the Yadav perplexities from M77 data was found as of October 2026; the 2020s studies use ICIT-derived corpora with a longer sign list (584 types in [Nair 2026, preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.17828)) and are context, not targets.

## A 20-line Witten-Bell model beats NLTK, and protocol moves perplexity as much as the model does

Witten-Bell in its interpolated form is p(w|h) = (c(h,w) + T(h) p_lower(w)) / (c(h) + T(h)), where T(h) is the number of distinct symbols seen after history h, and Chen and Goodman end the recursion in the **uniform distribution**, which is what guarantees no zero probabilities ([Chen & Goodman 1999](https://u.cs.biu.ac.il/~yogo/courses/mt2013/papers/chen-goodman-99.pdf)). For this project the matrix is 418 x 418: rows are the start token plus 417 signs, columns are 417 signs plus end-of-text. The start token is a context only and end-of-text is a predicted event only.

```python
from collections import Counter, defaultdict
BOS, EOS = "<s>", "</s>"

def fit(texts, V):                       # V = fixed 417-sign list, never derived from a fold
    uni, big = Counter(), defaultdict(Counter)
    for t in texts:
        s = [BOS] + list(t) + [EOS]
        uni.update(s[1:])                # BOS is never a predicted event
        for a, b in zip(s, s[1:]):
            big[a][b] += 1
    n_events = len(V) + 1                # signs + EOS = 418
    N, T = sum(uni.values()), len(uni)
    def p1(w):
        return (uni[w] + T / n_events) / (N + T)
    def p2(w, h):
        c = big.get(h)
        if not c:
            return p1(w)                 # unseen context
        return (c[w] + len(c) * p1(w)) / (sum(c.values()) + len(c))
    return p1, p2
```

This code was checked in a local test on a toy corpus (texts `abca`, `abd`, `cab`, `e`; vocabulary a to g), where every row summed to 1 and held-out text `abc` scored perplexity 4.04 counting end-of-text and 2.72 without. The row-sum assertion is the single most useful unit test, and a second one comes from the paper itself: before smoothing, P(b | a = 2) is non-zero only for b in {12, 14, 162, 176}, and smoothing must give the other signs small finite values ([Yadav et al. 2010, Fig. 16](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0009506)). The usual hand-written errors are counting the start token in the unigram, using a sign's raw count instead of its count as a context, using 417 rather than 418 in the uniform term, and building the vocabulary from the training fold.

One caveat governs the replication. **Yadav used the backoff form, and this code is the interpolated form.** SRILM states both: backoff weight n(a\*) / (n(a\*) + c(a)), with the same discounted estimate c(az) / (n(a\*) + c(a)) used either interpolated or as the backoff numerator ([SRILM smoothing notes](http://www.denizyuret.com/2007/12/srilm-ngram-smoothing-notes.html)). Chen and Goodman found that for Witten-Bell "the backoff version consistently outperforms the interpolated version", and that backoff Witten-Bell "performs extremely poorly for small training sets" relative to a Jelinek-Mercer baseline, with Kneser-Ney best overall ([Chen & Goodman 1999](https://u.cs.biu.ac.il/~yogo/courses/mt2013/papers/chen-goodman-99.pdf)). Those results come from word corpora with very large vocabularies, so their transfer to 417 signs is unproven. The sound plan is to implement both Witten-Bell forms, run absolute discounting or Kneser-Ney and a tuned add-k through the same folds, and report all of them. The interpolated model should land near the bigram value of 26.69 but is not expected to reproduce 26.09 and 25.26 at higher orders, where SRILM's backoff behaviour and count cut-offs, which the paper does not give, decide the result. The identical n = 4 and n = 5 values on texts averaging 4.5 signs show the higher orders are mostly backing off anyway, so the bigram matrix is the model that matters.

### NLTK's Witten-Bell returns infinity by design

The reviewer's warning about NLTK is half right. NLTK's `WittenBell.unigram_score` returns the maximum-likelihood unigram frequency, so the bigram weights match Chen and Goodman but the base case does not ([nltk/lm/smoothing.py](https://github.com/nltk/nltk/blob/develop/nltk/lm/smoothing.py)). NLTK documents the consequence as intended: a sentence with an unseen word "should result in infinite entropy because Witten-Bell is based ultimately on MLE" ([NLTK lm howto](https://www.nltk.org/howto/lm.html)). In local tests on NLTK 3.10.3 (2 October 2026), `WittenBellInterpolated` returned `inf` for a held-out text containing a sign with zero training count even when that sign was in an explicit closed vocabulary. With 112 of the 417 signs occurring once in the export, each of five folds holds roughly 22 hapax signs with no training occurrence, so **every fold would score infinite**. The crash reported in the still-open 2021 issue (`'int' object has no attribute 'N'`) did not reproduce on 3.10.3 ([nltk issue #2727](https://github.com/nltk/nltk/issues/2727)). Two quieter faults did appear in those tests: NLTK assigns probability to the start token as a predicted symbol (0.070 after `a` in the toy example), and a flat list of token strings passed to `entropy` is read as a list of n-grams and scored wrongly without an error. The accurate statement for the report is that NLTK's Witten-Bell backs off to an unsmoothed unigram and therefore does not remove zero probabilities; it is a design choice, not a bug. KenLM is no alternative, since it estimates only modified Kneser-Ney and raises `BadDiscountException` on small data ([kenlm issue #82](https://github.com/kpu/kenlm/issues/82)). SRILM is a credible independent cross-check but not worth a dependency.

### Fix the evaluation protocol before computing anything

Perplexity here depends on protocol as much as on the model. Chen and Goodman count the end-of-sentence token in the normaliser and not the start token ([Chen & Goodman 1999](https://u.cs.biu.ac.il/~yogo/courses/mt2013/papers/chen-goodman-99.pdf)); follow that for the headline figure and report the sign-only figure too, because end-of-text is roughly 18% of predicted events on the full export and is easy to predict, so counting it lowers perplexity. Fix the 417-sign vocabulary before splitting and do not map rare signs to an unknown class, which would merge 112 signs into one easy target. Split by distinct text, never by copy, token or bigram: duplicates across the split lower perplexity and flatter higher orders. Compute cross-entropy per fold, average that, and convert to perplexity at the end; report the spread across folds and seeds as descriptive, since folds share training data. Run a uniform baseline (perplexity exactly 418) and a smoothed unigram on the same folds and compare paired. Dropping test events with unseen signs to avoid an infinity is invalid, because it removes the hardest events.

**The baseline this milestone most needs is a position-aware zero-order model**, p(sign | position from start, position from end), smoothed the same way. In texts of four or five signs, strong initial and final preferences produce apparent bigram dependence without any sign-to-sign constraint. Unless the bigram model beats that baseline on held-out data, "Markov dependence" only restates "position matters". This is an argument from the structure of the data rather than a published Indus result, and no study was found that quantifies it, which makes it a contribution the project can make. The complementary test is a within-text shuffle (1,000 or more permutations preserving each text's length and sign multiset), with the p-value taken from the null distribution, which cancels small-sample bias.

Three analyses are invalid at this size and should be left out. The asymptotic likelihood-ratio test of Markov order has 416 squared, about 173,000, degrees of freedom against roughly 10,500 transitions, an expected count near 0.06 per cell. Stationary distributions and mixing times describe long sequences; a five-sign text makes four transitions, the chain with boundary tokens is absorbing, and an artificial loop from end to start just returns the unigram frequencies. Empty cells are not evidence of prohibited pairs. For pair association, rank by the log-likelihood ratio as Yadav did, test only observed pairs or pairs with expected count of about 5 or more, state the number of tests and apply Benjamini-Hochberg, and run it on de-duplicated texts. For entropy, the plug-in conditional entropy is biased downward by an amount that is not negligible here, so report plug-in, Miller-Madow and NSB or Chao-Shen side by side; NSB and Chao-Shen are the recognised small-sample benchmarks ([Hausser & Strimmer 2009](https://arxiv.org/abs/0811.3579)) and Rao's group used NSB for block entropy ([Rao et al. 2010](https://aclanthology.org/J10-4016.pdf)). Bootstrap over whole texts, not tokens. The formulas for Dunning's statistic, Benjamini-Hochberg and the entropy corrections were not re-read from the original papers for this report and should be checked against them before publication.

## Direction is tested on physical positions, not on reading order

Right-to-left reading was settled by physical evidence, not statistics. Signs are compressed at the left edge "as if the writer is running out of space", and the seal impression, not the carved seal, is what reads right to left ([Wikipedia, Indus script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_script)); signs also "spill out of an otherwise linear arrangement" at the left ([Ashraf & Sinha 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0190735)). B. B. Lal's 1966 stroke-overlap argument from Kalibangan sherds is the classic order-of-execution evidence, but it was available for this report only second-hand, so cite it from the original before relying on it. Left-to-right and boustrophedon exceptions exist, which makes right-to-left a majority convention.

The circularity trap is the concordance itself. Standard practice reverses the minority left-to-right sequences "so as to be oriented in the same direction as the majority" ([Ashraf & Sinha 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0190735)). In stored order every line therefore has its ender last by construction, and "enders concentrate at the end" holds whatever the true direction was. The one published direction statistic has this limitation for the Indus case. Ashraf and Sinha define ΔG = 2(GL − GR)/(GL + GR) from the Gini indices of sign usage at the left and right terminal positions, and the analogous ΔS from entropies; across 24 languages, left-to-right scripts give negative ΔG and right-to-left scripts positive, and the Indus value is positive, shown only in a figure with no number printed ([Ashraf & Sinha 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0190735)). They computed it on a normalised database.

**Forward-versus-backward bigram perplexity cannot rescue the test.** By the chain rule H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y|X) = H(Y) + H(X|Y), and for a maximum-likelihood bigram model with start and end tokens the forward and backward likelihoods of the training data are identical, because each sign occurs as often on the left of a pair as on the right. Any gap that appears with smoothing and held-out data reflects the estimator and where rare signs sit. Without boundary tokens the gap reduces to the difference between the first-sign and last-sign distributions, which is ΔS again. This is standard information theory rather than a cited Indus result; report a forward/backward number only as a secondary descriptive figure beside that identity. What is legitimately asymmetric is the terminal distributions: per-sign probabilities of being first, last or medial, distinct-successor versus distinct-predecessor counts, and the 82 versus 23 coverage figures ([Yadav et al. 2010](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0009506)).

The valid design undoes the normalisation. The export records a direction code per line; reversing the stored order for lines labelled right-to-left and keeping it for lines labelled left-to-right yields physical left-to-right order, and ΔG and ΔS are then computed on physical left versus physical right ends. An exploratory run on the project's own export ([pinned IM77 CSV](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/paragorth/Indus-/ed56b9dc293f26a4e6bddea36c9a394e6b211ff4/data/im77/im77_corpus_lines.csv)) gave, for 2,461 usable lines labelled right-to-left, ΔG = +0.195 and **ΔS = −0.395** (bootstrap 95% interval −0.423 to −0.369) against shuffle and mirror-half nulls within ±0.03; sign 342 was the physical-left terminal **802 times and the physical-right terminal 0 times**. For 210 lines labelled left-to-right the signs flipped: ΔG = −0.283 and ΔS = +0.324 (0.247 to 0.383). These are the project's unreviewed counts, not published figures, and that run resampled lines rather than texts and did not de-duplicate, so its intervals are too narrow.

Read the result in two layers. The majority result (concentrated enders on the physical left in 92% of directed lines) combined with Ashraf and Sinha's regularity points to right-to-left. It rests on two assumptions: that the export's `direction` field is a correct record of physical orientation in impression view, which has not been verified against the printed concordance, and that an analogy from words to Indus texts holds. It does not require knowing why Mahadevan chose each label. The flip in the labelled minority is where the reason for the label matters: it is a consistency check, and independent evidence only if Mahadevan assigned those labels from layout rather than from sign identity; the export does not record his reasons, and his 1977 introduction was not consulted, so **that residual circularity must be stated**. Four nulls answer four questions: a within-text shuffle tests whether any end asymmetry exists; mirror-reversing a random half of texts gives the zero-direction baseline; permuting the direction labels, stratified by object type and site, tests whether labels carry information about physical placement; and a label-free orientation fit, which finds the orientation of each line that maximises ender concentration and compares it with Mahadevan's labels, gives an agreement rate that uses no label at all. The last is a design suggestion, not a published method, and no published study was found that runs the statistic on physical positions split by label.

Robustness matters because the minority is confounded. In the export, left-to-right lines are 235 of 3,209 directed lines (7.3%), and 87 of them are miniature tablets, mostly from Harappa. Bootstrap over `text_no`, de-duplicate, drop single-sign, "symmetrical", "doubtful" and empty rows and the 155 rows oddly coded "no text" that nonetheless contain signs, and split by object type and site. Seals versus sealings is a useful internal control: both should give the same sign of ΔS if direction is coded in impression view. Report both ΔG and ΔS, because the two disagreed for Hawaiian and the entropy measure went the wrong way for Egyptian hieroglyphs with many rare terminal signs, a condition the Indus sign list shares ([Ashraf & Sinha 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0190735)). The claim to make is that sign placement is consistent with a right-to-left convention.

## The critics leave room for "structured" and none for "linguistic"

The 2009 to 2015 exchange ended without consensus, but it fixed what these statistics can show. Rao et al. plotted Indus conditional entropy near language samples and far from two controls, and concluded the results "increase the probability that the script represents language". The controls were artificial: one random and equiprobable, one with a unique successor per sign, which the reader learned only from the supplement ([Sproat 2010](https://aclanthology.org/J10-3012.pdf)). Sproat reproduced Rao's curve with "a model that has 400 elements with a perfect Zipf distribution" and conditionally independent bigrams, that is, **with no sequential structure at all**, and summarised the error as "random is not the same thing as random and equiprobable" ([Sproat 2010](https://aclanthology.org/J10-3012.pdf)). He also showed that the Lee et al. classifier for Pictish labels a kudurru deity-symbol corpus as logographic writing and a corpus of dice throws as syllabic writing, and that no decision rule was ever offered: the papers "present plots and invite the reader to eyeball them" ([Sproat 2010 reply](https://aclanthology.org/J10-4017.pdf)). Rao et al. replied that they never claimed sufficiency, "barring a full decipherment, one cannot prove either the linguistic or nonlinguistic thesis", and extended the argument to block entropy, which Sproat did not rebut in that exchange ([Rao et al. 2010](https://aclanthology.org/J10-4016.pdf)). Sproat's 2014 study in *Language* classified the Indus as non-linguistic on a repetition measure ([Sproat 2014](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/547119/summary)), and Rao et al. answered in 2015 that "we stand by our original findings" ([Rao et al. 2015](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language/article/abs/on-statistical-measures-and-ancient-writing-systems/D75CB72F35D752E4848CA69AE8E32888)). Both were available for this report at abstract level only, so treat each as one side's result.

Two unreviewed 2026 preprints sharpen the point for this milestone specifically. Raghavendra built a meaningful but non-phonological emblem system and reports that it "receives the same category as the Indus corpus on every criterion scored", with bigram perplexity falling 64.22 to 25.80 and 84.88% restoration by the 90%-mass rule, against the Indus 68.82 to 26.69 and 74%; his conclusion is that the measures "detect organization, predictability, concentration, direction, and model misfit; they do not, on their own, establish encoded speech" ([Raghavendra 2026, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02999)). He also concedes it is one synthetic system built after the literature was known. Nair reports that neither of two calibrated non-linguistic generators reproduces the full Indus profile and adds "this does not settle the linguistic question" ([Nair 2026, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.17828)). They disagree on whether non-linguistic baselines match and agree that the question is open. Some of their numbers were read through an automatic summariser, so check the PDFs before quoting.

For the project this converts into a claim-to-control rule. "Sign order is non-random" needs a frequency-matched memoryless baseline and a within-text shuffle, reported as an observed statistic against a null distribution with seeds and counts. "Context improves prediction" needs held-out perplexity against a unigram and a positional model on the same folds. "Positional regularities exist" needs a shuffle that preserves text lengths and sign frequencies. "The system resembles language" should not be claimed in either direction. Sproat's criterion for a valid test is a measure shown to "reliably distinguish" a large sample of known linguistic and non-linguistic systems ([Sproat 2010](https://aclanthology.org/J10-3012.pdf)), which a milestone on one corpus cannot supply. Restoration accuracy in particular measures the predictability of a notation and says nothing about semantics. Preferred wording is "consistent with", "departs from the null by" and "sequential and positional regularity"; avoid "syntax", "grammar", "words" and "linguistic structure". Even the n-gram authors' own preprint stopped at "a structured sign system showing features of a formal language" that "cannot conclusively establish that it encodes natural language" ([arXiv:0901.3017](https://arxiv.org/abs/0901.3017)). Sproat's small-sample warning also applies to the method itself: n-gram models are reliable on millions of tokens, not "a few hundred or few thousand symbols" ([Sproat 2010](https://aclanthology.org/J10-3012.pdf)).

## The export does not reproduce EBUDS, so the filter funnel is itself a result

EBUDS removes "all texts containing lost, damaged, illegible, or doubtfully read parts", removes texts spread across multiple lines, and keeps a single copy of repeated texts; multi-line texts go because it is unclear whether each line is a separate text and the cross-line reading order is ambiguous ([Yadav et al. 2010](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0009506)). The paper gives no per-step counts. Its one published check is the list of 40 signs absent from EBUDS: sign 374 (9 occurrences in M77), 237 (8), 282 and 390 (3 each), 324, 376 and 378 (2 each) and 33 hapaxes.

The project's export matches M77's headline totals and still misses EBUDS. Counted from the [pinned IM77 CSV](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/paragorth/Indus-/ed56b9dc293f26a4e6bddea36c9a394e6b211ff4/data/im77/im77_corpus_lines.csv), it has 3,573 non-empty lines in 2,906 texts and 417 sign types, as published, with 14,153 sign positions of which 781 are illegible markers (sign `0`), leaving **13,372 legible tokens**; the two totals describe the same data. Applying EBUDS-style rules and de-duplicating gives the following, all the project's own counts.

| Filter, then de-duplicate | Units | Tokens | Sign types |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single-line texts, no illegible, no doubtful | 1,291 | 6,099 | 347 |
| Single-line texts, no illegible, doubtful kept | 1,421 | 6,780 | 357 |
| All lines as units, no illegible, no doubtful | 1,673 | 7,448 | 393 |
| All lines as units, no illegible, doubtful kept | 1,849 | 8,313 | 398 |
| Published EBUDS | 1,548 | about 7,000 | 377 |

Either EBUDS used rules not fully stated or the export differs from Yadav's file. The project therefore reports "an EBUDS-style filter", publishes its funnel with texts, lines, tokens and types at every step, and treats the failure to reach 1,548 / 377 as a finding. Running the replication on two bracketing variants (the strict 1,291-text set and the 1,673-line set) shows how far the headline numbers move with the filter, which is more informative than a single near-match.

Each preparation choice has a defensible default. **Duplicates**: 567 texts (19.5%) are redundant copies in the export, concentrated in copper tablets (77% of which sit in a duplicate group) and miniature tablets (54%), against 16% of seals; one text occurs 22 times. Use de-duplicated texts as primary, all copies as a sensitivity check, and note that de-duplication shifts the object-type mix toward seals. The export cannot tell copies from one mould apart from independent attestations of a formula, a distinction Nair also flags ([Nair 2026, preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.17828v1)). **Illegible signs** (648 texts): dropping the text is cleanest; splitting at the gap manufactures false initial and final positions and corrupts exactly the positional statistics this milestone studies, so avoid it; a gap token that contributes no bigram and no position-from-end count keeps the most data for the full-corpus sensitivity run. **Doubtful readings** (423 texts): drop for the primary set and rerun with them kept, using the export's `doubtful_positions` field; they cannot serve as restoration ground truth, and Yadav's 74% was scored on masked signs in clean held-out texts, not on real doubtful readings. **Multi-line texts** (607, or 20.9%): one sequence per line with boundary tokens is the permissive default and dropping them is the EBUDS default; never count a bigram across a line or side break, since no source supports concatenation. **Single-sign texts** (69 texts, 215 lines) count toward frequencies and not toward transitions. **Sites**: Mohenjo-daro and Harappa hold 87% of texts, so every "Indus" statistic is mostly theirs; report those two separately, pool the rest, and draw nothing from 17 West Asian texts.

All results are conditional on the M77 sign list. Other inventories are much longer, 584 types on ICIT-derived data ([Nair 2026, preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.17828)) and 593 on the Wells list ([Sinha et al. 2010](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1005.4997)), every type-based statistic moves with that choice, and no published sensitivity analysis of the n-gram or entropy results across sign lists was found. One cheap check, collapsing signs below a frequency threshold into a rare class and reporting how each headline statistic moves, addresses it. The critics' complaint that the Rao result "can't be replicated even in principle unless you use exactly the same texts" ([Farmer, Sproat & Witzel 2009](https://safarmer.com/more.on.Rao.pdf)) sets the reporting standard: pin the corpus by commit and hash, publish surviving text IDs rather than sign sequences given the export's unclear licence, fix seeds and permutation counts, name each estimator, and state the unit of analysis. Several field semantics in the export remain unverified against the printed concordance.

## Conclusion

The most valuable output of Milestone 2 is probably not a matched perplexity table. No one has independently re-derived Yadav's numbers from M77, the export in hand cannot rebuild EBUDS, and the paper omits its split and SRILM settings, so a transparent near-replication with a published filter funnel and a stated list of what did not match would be a small but real addition to the literature. Three pieces of the design have no published precedent that this review found: a position-aware baseline that separates sequential from positional structure, a direction test on physical positions with a label-free check, and a filter-sensitivity band around every headline figure.

The debate also changes what a good result looks like. A bigram perplexity near 27 and a 90%-mass hit rate near 74% would confirm that the pipeline works, and a non-phonetic synthetic system reaches the same values, so the same numbers cannot carry a claim about language. The milestone succeeds if it shows how much of the predictability survives each control, and it should say outright that it makes no inference to language, non-language or meaning.
